If you've stared at a blank college essay prompt convinced that your life simply hasn't given you enough material to work with, you're not alone, and you're also not at a disadvantage. Admissions officers read thousands of essays about big, obvious moments: a championship game, a mission trip, a family tragedy. What they remember are the small, specific stories told with genuine reflection, not the size of the event described.

<a href="https://www.stjohns.edu/news-media/johnnies-blog/personal-essay-tips-for-college-admissions" target="_blank" rel="noopener">St. John's University's own admissions guidance</a> makes this point directly: even a story that seems ordinary on the surface can be powerful when it's presented with authenticity and genuine reflection. What matters to admissions readers is not the event itself, but how it shaped you.

<h2 id="why-ordinary-works">Why "Ordinary" Isn't a Weakness</h2>

The instinct to search for a dramatic life event comes from a reasonable place. It feels like admissions essays should match the scale of a major turning point. In practice, the opposite is often true. A small, specific story, told with real depth and reflection, demonstrates exactly what admissions officers are actually looking for: how you think, not just what has happened to you.

<div class="tip-callout">
  <strong>Tip:</strong> If you're comparing your life to a classmate's and feeling like yours is "less interesting," stop comparing subject matter and start comparing depth. A well-examined ordinary moment beats a shallow account of a dramatic one almost every time.
</div>

<h2 id="finding-your-angle">Finding Your Angle</h2>

Instead of searching for a single big event, try working backward from small, recurring details in your actual daily life:

- A habit or small ritual you've kept for years, and why you started it
- A minor, ongoing frustration you deal with regularly, and what it's taught you about patience or problem-solving
- A small responsibility you hold, at home, at work, or in your community, that most people wouldn't notice
- A specific, brief conversation that changed how you thought about something, even slightly

None of these need to sound impressive on their own. Their strength comes from how closely you examine them, not from their scale.

<div class="tip-callout">
  <strong>Tip:</strong> Write down five small, specific moments before you judge any of them. The moment you like best on first instinct is rarely the one that ends up working; the one you almost skipped usually has more to say.
</div>

<h2 id="structure">Structuring the Essay Around a Small Moment</h2>

Once you've picked your angle, resist the urge to cover a broad time span. Zoom in on a narrow window, sometimes a single afternoon or a single recurring habit, and spend most of your word count on reflection rather than narration.

A workable structure:

- **Open with a specific, concrete detail** from the moment itself, not a general statement about your life
- **Briefly set the scene**, just enough for the reader to understand the context
- **Spend the majority of the essay on what you noticed, thought, or felt**, and how that shifted or deepened over time
- **End by connecting it to who you are now**, without over-explaining the connection

The goal is depth over breadth. A single well-examined afternoon will almost always outperform a rushed summary of an entire year.

<h2 id="mistakes">Common Mistakes to Avoid</h2>

- Trying to cover too much time or too many events in one essay
- Explaining the "lesson learned" too directly instead of letting it emerge through specific details
- Choosing a topic because it sounds impressive rather than because you genuinely have something to say about it
- Spending most of the word count describing what happened rather than how you thought about it
