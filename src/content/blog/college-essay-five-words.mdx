If you've spent longer staring at a five-word prompt than you did on your entire personal statement, you're in good company. Something about the tiny word count makes it feel like there's no room for error, and that pressure often leads students to default to the safest, most generic words they can think of. That's usually the wrong instinct.

<h2 id="why-hard">Why This Short Prompt Feels So Hard</h2>

Longer essays give you room to build context, but a five-word answer has to work instantly. There's no space to explain or soften a word choice, which is exactly why students freeze up. The prompt isn't actually asking you to summarize your entire personality in five words; it's asking for five small, honest data points that add texture to the rest of your application.

<div class="tip-callout">
  <strong>Tip:</strong> Write down fifteen or twenty words before you narrow anything down. The first five words you think of are almost always the most generic ones; the interesting choices tend to show up after you've exhausted the obvious list.
</div>

<h2 id="avoid-generic">Avoiding Generic Words</h2>

Admissions officers read thousands of applications a year, and certain words show up constantly: hardworking, passionate, dedicated, intelligent, kind. None of these are false, but none of them are memorable either, because they could describe almost any applicant.

Instead, aim for words that are:

- **Specific to you**, not something that could apply to half the applicant pool
- **True**, not chosen because they sound impressive
- **A little unexpected**, revealing a side of you the rest of your application hasn't already covered

A word like "whimsical" or "meticulous" tells a reader something concrete. A word like "hardworking" tells them almost nothing they couldn't already guess from your transcript.

<h2 id="how-to-choose">How to Choose Your Five Words</h2>

A useful way to approach this prompt is to think about what the rest of your application has already told the reader, and then deliberately choose words that add something new.

- Reread your activities list and personal statement first, and note the qualities they already reveal
- Brainstorm freely without judging any word as "too weird" or "too small"
- Test each word by asking: would this genuinely surprise someone who only knows me from my transcript?
- Read your final five words together as a set. Do they feel like five different facts about you, or do two of them say the same thing in different language?

<a href="https://admission.stanford.edu/apply/first-year/apply.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Stanford's own application page</a>, for example, sets a clear minimum and maximum word count for each short answer response, which is a reminder that these prompts are read closely, not skimmed. Treat the constraint as a real creative challenge, not a box to fill quickly.

<h2 id="examples">What Strong Answers Look Like</h2>

Strong five-word answers tend to feel oddly specific rather than broadly flattering. Think less "ambitious, driven, curious, resilient, kind" and more a set of words that only really makes sense once you know the person: a word tied to a hobby nobody else in your family understands, a word that captures a very particular sense of humor, a word connected to a small daily habit.

If two of your five words are doing the same job, cut one and use the space for something that reveals a different angle entirely.
