Public administration is supposed to provide fair and consistent service to citizens, but the very institutions designed to ensure it can be a drag on service delivery. Public sector reform has been driven by the opposite extremes of order and speed for more than a hundred years. Max Weber's model of rational bureaucracy, with hierarchy, formal rules, and impersonal procedures, was created to increase predictability and diminish favoritism (Ortina et al. 2023). Just the same characteristics that ensured consistency now often result in agencies worldwide becoming slow and inflexible.

<!--split-->

This essay maintains that this is so, even when all concerned are fellow democrats. Nevertheless, public administration needs to be deliberately and effectively redesigned to be efficient without compromising the rule of law, which citizens need and rely on. The essay builds on recent research in digital government, performance measurement, and administrative theory to examine a traditional bureaucratic model, explore its shortcomings, and consider ways to close the gap between order and speed if the bureaucracy is to be reformed in the right way.

Weber's ideal-type bureaucracy is based on a clear division of labor, a clear chain of command, written regulations, and appointments based on merit rather than personal connections. The principles were revolutionary at the time as they abolished patronage and arbitrary decision-making and instead adopted a uniform set of rules for all citizens. Government efficiency, however, is not as easy to measure as government output does not have a market price, as noted by Ortina et al. (2023). A hospital, a licensing office, or a tax authority can't just come up with some formula for comparing revenue against cost as a private firm can. The value of a public service is not always tangible or confined to the boundaries of an individual or corporation. Rather, efficiency in public administration is defined as the ratio of service costs to the costs of the resources used to produce them. The above definition describes the need for the development of bureaucratic rules. In its absence, a profit signal, a formal procedure, was the most important protection against waste, corruption, and unequal treatment of citizens. The model succeeded in establishing trust and consistency within institutions and remains the foundation of most modern public agencies, ranging from courts to welfare offices.

<!--split-->

Bureaucracy is often seen as failing to achieve efficiency in everyday life; however, for these reasons, it has many strengths. Too many steps, too much time taken, and do not make citizens happy because they don't benefit from the system. This issue is clearly demonstrated in the Czech Republic by the study of data mailboxes by Dušek (2023). It was implemented to use technology to make it easier to communicate among citizens, businesses, and government bodies, with the stated intent to make communication faster and more transparent throughout the country. But as Dušek records, it was complicated to register, technical problems frequently occurred, and some 40% of the new mailboxes were never used because citizens were confused or unable to register.

A national audit body found that the responsible ministry was ineffective at raising public awareness or making the service simple enough to achieve the intended goals. The case illustrates that the digitization of a bureaucratic process does not necessarily make it efficient. If legislation is inflexible and agencies don't put the end user first, technology puts the same procedure on screen instead of eliminating it. In larger systems, administrative, whether digital or paper, goal displacement, which occurs when following the rule takes priority over achieving the goal for which it was created, is always present and increases with each level of oversight added without a corresponding decrease in older, redundant oversight.

<!--split-->

The radical reforms over the last 30 years have sought to address inefficiencies without completely eradicating bureaucratic accountability. According to Ciancarini, Giancarlo, and Grimaudo (2024), digital transformation in the public sector is more about changing organizational culture and mindset than simply introducing new technologies. Their research focuses on the need for cultural, organizational, and legal changes to go hand in hand with the implementation of a new digital system; otherwise, the problems will continue in a new form. This is a lesson straight out of the Czech case about the constraints of technology without institutional change. In addition to introducing technology, governments have implemented formal performance structures to measure and stimulate efficiency more discipline.

Ortina et al. (2023) outline the Common Assessment Framework developed by the EU, as well as performance indicators used in the United Kingdom, Australia, and several states in the United States, including average transaction costs, successful completion of services, and overall uptake by citizens. These frameworks provide specific facts and details about the places where the bureaucracy is failing to serve the citizens, and this allows the director to specific ways to change the bureaucracy rather than wholesale deregulation that might erode the protections the bureaucracy provides. The results of this approach, when done well, are single-window service portals, digitized tax filing, and streamlined licensing systems, which limit the steps the citizen has to take while ensuring that the public is protected from misuse of public resources.

The need to balance structure and flexibility will be a major issue for public administration in the future. Technological solutions are not an answer to the problem of bureaucracy, since citizens' immunity from favoritism and legal consistency is ensured by hierarchy, written rules, and impersonal treatment, which are essential for long-term governance and the institutional memory. The idea is smart bureaucracy maintaining a necessary range of oversight and pruning away any process that doesn't add value and costs money. It is worth remembering the advice provided by Drechsler (2022). He writes about the changing nature of public administration as new technologies, such as artificial intelligence and algorithmic tools, transform the profession, and cautions that the digital tools can reinforce institutionalized habits or can create opportunities for truly new administrative thinking. This is a warning to anyone interested in bureaucratic reform. But it is not technology that determines the actual outcome, it depends on leadership and organizational culture. Agencies that understand digital tools as a vehicle for reimagining how they respond to citizens' needs are more likely to succeed. By contrast, agencies that automate existing paper processing often re-create the same delays in a digital format, failing to bring real change but rather a "digital bureaucracy."

Bureaucracy and efficiency don't have to be in conflict, but it takes intentional change, not blind acceptance of new apps and old routines. Weber's model provided order and fairness in public administration, which remain important for any functioning state. The tools available to efficiently deliver the services, in that order, have changed. The Czech data mailbox system and the overall research on digital transformation and performance measurement provide a clear answer. The prospects for administrative reform, as led by cultural change, evidence-based evaluation, and real consideration of end users, are more realistic and sustainable than either a traditional, fixed way of working or blind faith in technology. Governments that spend equally on culture and technology will have the best chance of closing the wide gap between bureaucracy and administration that has existed for years.
