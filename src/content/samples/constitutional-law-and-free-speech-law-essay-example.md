The First Amendment declares that Congress shall make no law abridging the freedom of speech, yet the Supreme Court has never treated this guarantee as absolute. Jeffrey Howard's entry on freedom of speech in the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy offers a useful lens for understanding why. Howard distinguishes between external constraints, in which speech is balanced against outside values such as safety or dignity, and internal constraints, in which the very values that justify free speech, including autonomy and democratic self-governance, generate limits from within. This essay argues that American free speech doctrine reflects both models at once, calibrating protection according to the value and harm potential of the speech in question, from early incitement cases through the unsettled frontier of digital platforms.

<!--split-->

The foundational limit on speech emerged in Schenck v. United States in 1919, where Justice Oliver Wendell Holmes articulated the clear and present danger test. Holmes reasoned that speech creating a clear and present danger of substantive evils Congress had a right to prevent could be restricted, famously comparing such speech to falsely shouting fire in a crowded theater. Although later abandoned as too permissive of suppression, this case established a durable principle: consequence and context matter as much as content in determining whether speech deserves protection.

That principle matured into the Brandenburg v. Ohio standard of 1969, which narrowed government power dramatically. Under Brandenburg, advocacy of unlawful action can only be punished when it is directed toward inciting imminent lawless action and is likely to produce it. This two-part test protects even radical or offensive political rhetoric, reflecting a judicial preference for counter-speech over censorship.

Clay Calvert's analysis of Richard Spencer's 2017 visit to the University of Florida illustrates how narrow this standard is in practice. Calvert shows that Spencer deliberately stopped short of the incitement line, remaining legally untouchable under Brandenburg even as his presence collapsed civil discourse on campus and imposed enormous security costs on the university. Calvert's case study demonstrates that a speaker can be genuinely disruptive to institutional order without ever forfeiting constitutional protection, exposing the real-world stakes behind an abstract legal test.

<!--split-->

Obscenity occupies different territory altogether. Miller v. California established a three-part test asking whether the average person applying contemporary community standards would find the work appeals to prurient interest, whether it depicts sexual conduct in a patently offensive way under state law, and whether it lacks serious literary, artistic, political, or scientific value. Unlike the incitement test, which asks about likely consequences, Miller asks about content and community judgment, confirming that different categories of speech receive different levels of scrutiny depending on their perceived value.

New York Times v. Sullivan addressed the collision between reputation and free expression. Public officials suing for defamation must prove actual malice, meaning the defendant spoke falsely with knowledge of falsity or reckless disregard for the truth. This standard gives public debate breathing room, tolerating honest error to protect legitimate criticism of government. Rather than eliminating either right, the Court adjusted the burden of proof, a recurring technique in constitutional balancing.

Texas v. Johnson extended protection to expressive conduct, holding that flag burning as political protest could not be criminalized simply because it offended onlookers. United States v. O'Brien nonetheless permits regulation of conduct containing a nonspeech element under intermediate scrutiny. The distinction turns on whether a law targets the expressive message itself or serves an interest unrelated to suppressing that message.

<!--split-->

Eugene Volokh's analysis in the Harvard Law Review Forum extends this doctrinal architecture into the platform age. In Moody v. NetChoice, the Court held that social media platforms exercise First Amendment protected editorial discretion over their curated feeds, much like a newspaper choosing which submissions to publish, though it left unresolved whether individual post removals or direct messaging receive identical protection. In Murthy v. Missouri, the Court avoided ruling on whether government pressure on platforms to remove content violates the First Amendment, resolving the case on standing grounds instead. In Lindke v. Freed, the Court grappled with when a government official's social media activity counts as state action rather than private expression. Volokh's central point is that private power over speech, not merely government power, now sits at the center of constitutional debate, and that the traditional lines between content based and content neutral regulation, and between government and private action, are being tested by technology the framers could not have anticipated.

Incitement, obscenity, defamation, symbolic conduct, and platform speech all reveal the same underlying structure: protection is calibrated rather than absolute. Returning to Howard's framework, modern doctrine increasingly blends external balancing against harm with the internal recognition that unrestrained speech can undermine the very democratic and truth-seeking values that justify protecting it. Understanding constitutional free speech therefore requires abandoning any assumption of unlimited expression, and instead appreciating a deliberate, evolving balance that now stretches from courtroom precedent into the architecture of digital platforms, a balance that will keep shifting as new technologies test old constitutional lines.
