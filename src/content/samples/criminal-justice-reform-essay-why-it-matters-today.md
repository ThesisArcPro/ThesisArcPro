More people in the United States are held captive than anywhere else in the world, and this has a significant impact on millions of lives annually. There has been much debate about prisons over the years, and they are still overcrowded, sentencing is still unequal, and ex-offenders often find it difficult to make a new life after prison. This is no coincidence. They result from structural decisions that are a part of American law and policy (Garland, 2023). Those imprisoned are not the only ones who suffer from mass incarceration, as social and economic costs of incarceration ripple through families, neighborhoods and communities. Today, criminal justice reform is important because too many people are incarcerated, sentencing is unequal, and rehabilitation is still not delivering results for people, their families, and their communities.

The trend for America to rely on incarceration was not solely the result of increasing crime. The nation's political economy, with centuries of racial exclusion and a low level of social safety net, drives legislators toward punishment versus prevention, according to Garland (2023). The United States, in contrast, has repeatedly been the default nation to send citizens to jail when dealing with social problems, as other wealthy democracies have done by investing in housing, health, and education as a means of dealing with the causes of crime. The result was mandatory minimum sentences, intense policing in low-income communities, and a steadily expanding prison population over the last five decades, well outpacing that of other developed countries with similar crime rates. The effects are far-reaching and reach beyond the people behind bars. A system that is funded with taxpayers' money, but costs billions of dollars a year, and facilities that are overcrowded with not enough services. Many people are now in jail for nonviolent crimes, and the question being asked is whether long sentences enhance public safety or if they simply result in the incarceration of those who could be better served through community-based alternatives. This system continues, argues Garland (2023), because it reflects political priorities instead of what has led to a reduction in crime rates. Changing the laws surrounding sentencing, eliminating mandatory sentencing for nonviolent crimes, and redirecting resources to prevention programs would help begin to balance the system. If nothing else happens, the U.S. will remain the world's leader in incarceration rates, and other indicators of public safety and community wellbeing will lag.

The inequities of sentencing add to over-incarceration. Black and Latino defendants are more likely to be sentenced to longer terms than white defendants for similar crimes and, when they cannot afford bail or longer at trial, are more likely to be offered plea deals they would not voluntarily accept. These inequities undermine consumer confidence in the judicial system and deepen the crisis of trust with respect to fairness, especially in communities that are already distrustful of the judicial system. This is a problem that is being acknowledged in recent legislation. Haber et al. (2024) outline several bipartisan bills that are rooted in the First Step Act, such as the First Step Implementation Act, which would expand sentencing relief for those convicted prior to the enactment of the act, and the Eliminating a Quantifiably Unjust Application of the Law Act that would address persistent federal sentencing disparities. These proposals demonstrate a commitment to reform, not just a desire; lawmakers both left and right agree that the status quo is unfair and inequitable. While Haber et al. (2024) describe these reforms as health reforms rather than legal, they still have a direct impact on physical and mental health outcomes for incarcerated individuals as well. Reducing inequality in sentencing is thus a two-fold objective. It brings fairness to a system that unfairly punishes some communities and diminishes the cumulative impact of too-long sentences on already-disadvantaged communities. One of the most obvious avenues for a more just criminal justice system is provided by continued legislation, which is based on evidence and not political rhetoric.

The justice system frequently isn't ready for these individuals when they're released. Mourão et al (2025) conducted a review of 38 studies and identified that stable employment, housing and social support are the most powerful factors that contribute to successful reintegration, but that during incarceration, few prisons provide access to education, job training or social support for families. If this is not done, many people will be released from prison. They are left with the same instability that led them to commit their crimes and are saddled with a criminal record that makes it difficult to find housing, jobs and even professional licenses in many states. Cloud et al. (2023) further note that incarceration exacerbates health problems that can make reintegration challenging; often, chronic illness, mental health issues and substance use disorders are untreated in prison. Current practice does not equip people to re-enter society, but rather to be less ready to do so than when they arrived. Only a small proportion of prisoners receive these programmes. Still, they have shown to have a strong impact on the outcomes, as reported by Mourão et al. (2025), who found that those programmes that provide vocational training, counselling, and regular contact with the family have the greatest impact. One of the starkest discrepancies between what the research reveals and what prisons actually deliver is the failure to offer the kind of therapeutic services that effectively treat substance abuse. One of the biggest disconnects between the research literature and prison services is a lack of providing therapeutic services that are proven to be effective in treating substance abuse. The factors that increase the risk for reincarceration in this study could be directly mitigated through improved opportunities for education, health and reentry planning, reducing the cycle of re-entering prison.

Those who oppose criminal justice reform say that diminishing sentences or increasing early release programs could be harmful to public safety because criminals might be released into communities with inadequate deterrents. This may be an important consideration because public safety is a legitimate objective of a justice system. But there is no evidence that tougher sentencing leads to a safer community. While public health initiatives, including decarceral policies, can lower reincarceration rates without increasing crime, they must include public policy reforms that tackle the root causes of criminal behavior that are related to health and social issues (Cloud et al., 2023). In states that have done so, they have seen no corresponding spike in crime rates, thus challenging the notion that long sentences deter crime, at least when compared to the promise of better housing, employment and health care. The evidence available is a testament to the fact that public safety and criminal justice reform are not mutually exclusive objectives, and that they can be achieved together if criminal justice reform is structured well and adequately resourced for implementation.

The prosecution of crime is important because the current criminal justice system is broken in several ways. Over-incarceration costs public resources, without commensurate safety dividends; sentencing differentials erode trust in the criminal justice system; and insufficient rehabilitation programs keep many individuals in the system. All these factors exacerbate one another: in an inequitable system, unfair sentencing compounds an already overburdened system, and in an overfilled system, there are fewer resources available to rehabilitate inmates in programs that reduce reoffending. The research presented in this document demonstrates that reform is sorely needed and also attainable. Policy, community, and criminal justice practitioners should focus on sentencing equity, increasing rehabilitation opportunities, and funding the services and supports necessary for reentry success upon release from prison. It is only until we have a system of justice that truly protects our safety and dignity as human beings that the United States can become a truly just society.
