The criminal justice system is seen as one whole process rather than three distinct processes. Law enforcement, courts, and corrections are all interdependent, and each one relies on the effectiveness of the previous link in the chain. A police investigation influences the evidence presented in court. The sentencing determination is what admits people into the correctional system and what conditions they must comply with while incarcerated. The results of incarceration, rehabilitative or otherwise, impact whether that person will be returned to the system or not. This essay suggests that the quality of the criminal justice system is more a function of the coordination of these three stages as they are confronted with pressure than of their respective strengths. If one stage of the system underperforms, either due to strain on resources, delay in a procedure, or because punishment is disproportionate to the rehabilitation of an offender, it has a knock-on effect throughout the system, which results in case backlogs, tougher sentences, and reoffending.

The criminal justice system is entered through the police. This is where officers investigate crimes, collect evidence, and make arrests that ultimately get the process going. This stage takes place before any judicial control, so it relies heavily on the discretion of the officers. Decisions made by officers between issuing citations, arresting, or handling a situation informally are those that cannot be completely reversed in later phases of the system. This discretion is not always a bad thing, but it can create variations that become more significant with distance downstream.

This contrasts with community-oriented policing and enforcement-heavy strategies. The community policing models focus on establishing trust with the community and working to prevent conditions that lead to crime, whereas the models that focus on the police presence focus on arrest. Agencies that tend to go to the end of the continuum typically create more caseload for courts and corrections. At the same time, the outcome of public safety is comparable to the other end of the spectrum. The workload and pressure of courts and corrections as part of the process will also be impacted by decisions made by law enforcement.

This is not a typical strain. Coordination between correctional agencies and local law enforcement agencies was cited as a critical, but sometimes underprepared, aspect of operational planning during the COVID-19 pandemic by Novisky, Tostlebe, Pyrooz, and Sanchez (2023). The police are not alone. It regulates the size of its staff, its training and training methods, and its procedures; these become inputs which are taken up by the courts and corrections, whether they can or not.

The analytical significance of law enforcement lies in its role as a filter. Decisions made during the investigation and arrest process will decide whether cases will move forward, progress at a swift pace, and be strong enough for evidence. The impact of this filter is felt later if it is inconsistent, for example, because of under-resourcing, poor policy formulation, or discretionary bias, and is manifested in case backlogs, contested prosecutions, or poor plea negotiations. Law enforcement is the first line of the system, and their mistakes are not easily forgotten. As cases advance towards adjudication, they are compounded.

Adjudicative core of the criminal justice system: the courts decide guilt and sentences. The actual situation in today's courts, however, is that the formal trial is the exception, and not the norm. Most cases are plea bargained, which is expedient but concerns issues of justice. This dynamic was compounded during the COVID-19 pandemic (Baldwin, Eassey, and Brooke, 2020) due to court closures and the health risks associated with the pandemic, which led to pressure for defendants to accept plea deals to avoid being incarcerated in jail. Their study also provides a glimpse of how the courts adapted to virtual hearings and remote arguments to keep going, showing the adaptability and fragility of the court infrastructure's capacity to cope with the strain.

Viglione, Peck, and Frazier (2023) provide a more detailed analysis of how case processing was impacted by the pandemic era and resulted in significant backlogs. What they found is that any operational stress, even short-term, can create delays that last long after the initial disruption, as court systems typically cannot deal with both a backlog and an ongoing demand for services at the same time. Direct repercussions of this are to defendants awaiting trial, especially those detained awaiting trial, and to victims awaiting resolution.

These delays have implications beyond an administrative issue. In eight studies involving analysis of more than 160,000 real punishment decisions, Kundro, Nurmohamed, Kakkar, and Affinito (2023) discovered that punishments for crimes that are delayed from their occurrence to resolution are more severe in the eyes of third parties, such as the judge. This is because delays are seen as "unfair" delays and lead decision makers to take this into account with increased severity, even though the defendant has not been at fault for the delay. It is an important discovery for the analysis of the courts since it demonstrates that inefficiency in the procedures does not just slow the system down. Deliberately skews the magnitude of consequences. A defendant whose case is delayed because of backlog, lack of staffing, or because of the pandemic disruption may end up with a stiffer sentence solely because of the delay, apart from the facts of the case.

Corrections are the last formal step in the criminal justice system, including incarceration, probation, and parole. It is said to serve a dual purpose: that of punishment and to bring about rehabilitation, although in practice the pursuit of these two purposes often relies on scarce resources. To explore the impact of the pandemic on prison operations, Novisky et al. (2023) surveyed 31 state correctional agencies and held focus groups with 62 correctional staff. They discovered that rehabilitative and educational programming was one of the first services to go on the chopping block when strain occurred, as 86 percent of agencies responding to the survey indicated their programming was reduced. This was exacerbated by staffing difficulties, where agencies were having trouble recruiting and retaining staff and dealing with infection control, quarantine, and fewer numbers of in-care people.

This discovery is not only relevant to the pandemic but also to health matters as a whole. It shows that rehabilitative programming, despite the fact that it is at the center of correctional philosophy, is considered disposable as resources become scarce in facilities. This has implications directly related to recidivism. In a thorough meta-review of incarceration research with proper causal designs, Loeffler and Nagin (2022) concluded that incarceration itself does not have a consistent impact on reoffending. They did find, however, a clear pattern that settings where rehabilitative programming is emphasized have a lower rate of recidivism than settings where rehabilitative programming is not emphasized have a higher rate of recidivism. The impact of pretrial detention was more consistently negative on future behavior.

All of this indicates that corrections are more of a variable outcome than a fixed institution, depending on whether facilities will be able to maintain programming under pressure. Crisis conditions or understaffing can cause correctional systems to create the conditions and produce the outcomes that they aim to avoid. Thus, under-resourced corrections lead to reoffending, generating new caseloads for law enforcement and the courts.

These three aspects don't work out alone. Court caseloads are based on discretionary decisions by law enforcement. As courts delay, as shown by Kundro et al. (2023), court delays skew the severity of sentencing beyond the characteristics of a case. As demonstrated by Novisky et al. (2023) and Loeffler and Nagin (2022), correctional strain is a predictor of whether imprisonment will lead to an increase or decrease in subsequent crime. A single theme binds together all three stages: the continued efforts for punitive efficiency versus rehabilitative investment. Systems with a focus on speed and containment always result in poorer outcomes over the long term, even when tested under stress, compared to rehabilitative systems.

The criminal justice system is as good as its least effective link. The ability to integrate investment in law enforcement, courts, and corrections — especially rehabilitative capacity — is a more effective way to reduce recidivism and restore public trust than spending more money on efficiency.
