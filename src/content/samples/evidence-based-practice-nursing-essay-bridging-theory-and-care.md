Healthcare professionals rely on evidence-based practice (EBP) as the backbone of contemporary nursing, guiding them from textbook learning to bedside decisions. Evidence-based practice (EBP) is the foundation of modern nursing and empowers the practice of moving from textbook learning to decision-making at the bedside. It encourages nurses to use the most current evidence, along with their clinical skills and patients' needs and desires, in making decisions rather than relying solely on habit, intuition, or tradition. Theoretically, integration should result in improved and safer patient care in all units and specialties. But, in reality, there is a significant divide between what nurses can provide at the bedside and what the goals of EBP are. This mismatch is an important question for analysis: Given the value placed on evidence-based care, why is it not used uniformly? This Essay explores that disconnect, unpacks the meaning of EBP in practice, deconstructs the difference between EBP and other related concepts, and illuminates the realities of clinical practice by examining what it shows about the disparity between knowledge and care. The analysis is based on four recent sources of conceptual, professional and empirical views. It proposes that the barrier is structural and not individual, is in systems of support and not a lack of knowledge of nurses.

<!--split-->

In order to meaningfully analyze EBP, it needs to be defined precisely. Grys (2022) provides a helpful conceptual framework that distinguishes the activities of EBP from two distinct activities that are often mistaken for EBP: research and quality improvement. Research creates new evidence by systematically investigating the evidence, quality improvement uses existing evidence to improve local processes, and EBP uses the best current evidence to address a patient or practice problem. Grys (2022) represents these relationships graphically with the concept of overlapping domains, highlighting that the three processes share some aspects but have their own unique roles within healthcare improvement. The distinction is important analytically because failing to separate EBP from research or quality improvement results in the misuse of training and resources, in which an implementation dilemma is treated as a knowledge generation dilemma, but the solutions are quite different. Considering EBP as an application process, rather than a discovery process, poses the question that nursing programs and hospitals should ask: "Why is there not enough evidence at the bedside.

This reframing is directly related to the nursing profession's standards. Brunt and Morris (2023) define EBP as combining the best evidence, clinician expertise and client needs in the context of the wider practice environment. In the nursing professional development context, this places EBP in a different category than a skill; it's a competency, and people who practice EBP must be advocates for scientific inquiry. Brunt and Morris (2023) also point to the hierarchy of evidence, which places the category of 'expert opinion' at the lower end of the spectrum and systematic reviews and meta-analyses at the top. The hierarchy provides nurses with a working instrument to evaluate the quality of the sources. Still, at the same time, it reveals a shortcoming: the ability to rank evidence does not equate to knowing where to find it, interpret it and apply it in a timely fashion. The expectation of professionals is clear, but the structures that help nurses achieve this are often not well developed.

<!--split-->

The theory-practice gap is most apparent, and qualitative research is important for complementing the depth of analysis. In a study by Mohamed et al. (2024), 64 nurses in Saudi Arabia participated in focus group discussions to identify their conceptual understanding of EBP and their ability to articulate the benefits of EBP, concluding that most participants had a conceptual understanding of EBP, but were not able to translate this conceptual knowledge into consistent clinical practice. The study highlighted organizational barriers, such as a lack of institutional support and a lack of access to research databases, as well as individual barriers, such as low confidence in interpreting statistical results. Importantly, nurses also identified facilitators that helped to implement; these included: structured mentorship, continuous training and clear institutional expectations. The findings from this Essay support an analytical claim that the theory-to-practice gap is not just one of lack of knowledge on the part of nurses. It is a systems issue because people are willing to use the evidence, and yet there is no supportive organizational system in place. This analysis of the gap from this perspective helps redirect the focus from assigning blame to individuals to holding institutions accountable, which has real meaning for how hospitals can design EBP support programs.

Clinical setting is also a factor high-acuity settings provide an excellent example of the difference with respect to EBP application, according to the results of a survey of 202 intensive care nurses in Lithuania by Šabanė, Vimantaitė, and Jakuška (2022). More experienced nurses uniformly employed evidence-based practice interventions more than less experienced nurses. Among the more experienced nurses, those who had better access to current guidelines applied them more consistently. The finding is analytically important because the length of stay in an intensive care unit is expected to be the most evidence-based in the hospital, with the severity of patients and the outcomes being so visible. However, if this is not the case, then it implies that inconsistency extends beyond the world of under-resourced and/or general care settings. It's a part of nursing practice overall, and it applies even in the most critical areas where there's the most pressure and scrutiny. The Lithuanian results support the conclusion of Mohamed et al. (2024) that while motivation is important, the access and experience of the clinician or provider play a large part in the chances of reaching the patient with evidence. Both studies indicate that geography and the type of healthcare system do not explain as much as the institutional support for evidence use or not.

<!--split-->

These four sources combine to form a consistent analysis. Grys (2022) provides the conceptual clarity in understanding what EBP is and how it is different from related processes that can be confused with EBP. Brunt and Morris (2023) place that in the context of professional standards and the evidence hierarchy, to situate the concept as a formal expectation and not a discretionary practice. Then, Mohamed et al. (2024) and Šabanė et al. (2022) provide empirical evidence indicating that the main barrier to implementing EBP is not the nurses' understanding of EBP but the organizational structure or lack thereof that supports nurses' ability to implement EBP. These sources suggest that bridging the theory-practice gap would involve three coordinated activities: more precise institutional delineation of the roles of EBP, continued funding of training and access to databases, and mentored EBP pairing of less experienced with more experienced nurses in their knowledge and skills in evidence appraisal.

The implications go beyond the individual hospitals. With an increasing emphasis on patient outcomes as a measure of healthcare effectiveness, EBP serves as the pathway between healthcare research and healthcare outcomes. The importance of the evidence base is rendered moot at the bedside unless there is an intentional bridging plan in place. Nursing education and continuing professional development have a special responsibility here, as they form a nurse's early relationship to the research literature, and also ensure the continued availability of the latest research evidence over a nurse's lifetime.

<!--split-->

Finally, evidence-based practice in nursing is not a single skill, but is best viewed as a negotiation between those things we know and those things we do. All four sources reviewed in this section conclude that nurses in general have the conceptual understanding needed to practice EBP. Still, organizational systems, mentorship, and access to quality evidence make the difference between being conceptual and being put into practice in terms of the care provided to patients. So, bridging theory and care is not so much an education of the nurse, but rather an education of the system that enables existing knowledge to do its job. Future research will be useful in identifying specific interventions, such as protected nurse time for evidence review or nurse mentoring arrangements, that are more likely to lead to consistent improvements in patient outcomes from nurses' knowledge. Until there is a similar seriousness to those systems as to clinical training, there will be a chasm between what nursing knows and what it does at the bedside.
