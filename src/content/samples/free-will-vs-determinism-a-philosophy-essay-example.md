One of the most persistent puzzles in philosophy is the problem of free will. Free will is the capacity to choose through agency and control, while determinism holds that every event is the necessary consequence of prior events combined with the laws of nature. If every choice is fixed in advance by conditions that precede it, genuine freedom appears to be ruled out. Yet rejecting determinism creates a different obstacle: without determination, a choice risks being random rather than controlled. This essay works through that tension between necessity and chance. It argues that determinism poses a real problem for free will, but that indeterminism does not solve it either. A more convincing account has to explain how rational deliberation and agent-level control matter, without collapsing human action into either a foreordained chain of events or unexplained randomness. The distinction is not simply about how causes operate, but about how causes can work through an agent's own deliberative capacities, and that distinction matters for how we understand complicated human decisions today.

When a person's complete physical and mental state, the laws of nature, and the prior condition of the universe jointly determine what that person will do, it becomes hard to say the person could have done otherwise. Tadić (2026) frames this as a version of the consequence argument: neither the past nor the laws of nature are within any individual's power, so once those conditions are fixed, free action becomes difficult to account for. Van Miltenburg (2022) raises a related incompatibilist worry, namely that deterministic causation threatens the kind of control that free action is supposed to involve.

This argument rests on a distinction between ordinary choice and ultimate authorship. A person can deliberate, hold desires, and act purposefully, yet determinism seems to push the causal chain outside the person entirely. An agent's reasons and decisions are themselves required by prior conditions, so simply pointing to those reasons may not be enough to establish freedom. Still, this conclusion depends on a particular picture of causation and agency; determinism does not automatically mean that the reasons behind an action are not genuinely the agent's own.

Van Miltenburg (2022) challenges a simple picture of causation by proposing an Anscombean account of free action, in which intentional action sits at the center of the analysis rather than the distinction between causation and deterministic necessitation alone. Actions can be explained by their purpose as well as by a description of physical events, and an agent can act for reasons. The movement of an arm is a neurological event, but the movement of an arm to make a chess move is also an act of intention, judgment, and practical reasoning.

This view leaves room for a compatibilist picture that does not rule out meaningful agency. If a person weighs reasons, forms an intention, and carries out an action in line with that intention, the action can still be attributed to the person even though it has causes. Cova and Martinez (2025) supply relevant evidence from experimental philosophy: participants' judgments about free will and moral responsibility under deterministic conditions depend partly on how well they actually understand determinism. This does not establish compatibilism on its own, but it does show that determinism, agency, and responsibility are not automatically incompatible.

Rejecting determinism, however, is not by itself enough to secure free will, and this is the central difficulty for libertarian accounts. Suppose a person chooses between two options and the choice is not fixed by anything that came before it. Absent a convincing further explanation, the outcome can look like it was selected by chance, and an exercise of freedom is not the same thing as an agent's lack of control over randomness. Van Miltenburg (2022) makes a similar point: it is not enough to swap probabilistic or indeterministic causation in for deterministic causation and simply assume that probabilistic causation grants agency.

Potter and Mitchell (2026) examine this problem through what is often called the luck objection. The objection holds that if an action is genuinely undetermined, something external to the agent must help settle which of several possibilities becomes actual, making the action a matter of luck rather than freedom. Potter and Mitchell question the objection because it rests on an overly narrow conception of human decision-making.

They point out that decision-making is a biological process that is responsive and reason-guided, drawing on multiple considerations, cognitive processing, goals, and feedback. An indeterministic process need not resemble a random event unrelated to the agent. Decision-making systems can shape behavior without simply injecting noise or uncertainty. Even if the underlying microscopic processes are not fully deterministic, an agent can still exercise control by weighing reasons and acting to achieve goals. This response undercuts the assumption that an undetermined choice must be a random one.

Tadić (2026) extends the discussion into neuroscience and contemporary science more broadly. Research in the tradition of Libet and related studies has often been read as showing that brain activity precedes conscious awareness of a decision, which some take as evidence that the brain "decides" before the conscious agent does. Tadić argues that such results do not conclusively show that agency is illusory, largely because of the limits involved in interpreting neural signals for simple motor tasks and extending those conclusions to complex decisions, experimental design, and timing methods.

Scientific evidence may rule out particular theories of free will, but it is not sufficient to resolve every metaphysical question about agency. Tadić therefore adopts a cautious, naturalistic stance: the current state of physics, neuroscience, and cognitive science does not conclusively establish or rule out free will. Neuroscience should not be treated as proof of determinism, and scientific uncertainty should not be treated as proof of libertarian freedom.

Taken together, these four sources point toward a debate that should not be confined to either deterministic necessity or unexplained chance. Van Miltenburg (2022) argues for a more nuanced account of causation in agency, one that makes room for rational and intentional action. Potter and Mitchell (2026) support this by showing that meaningful control is achievable through indeterministic biological processes. Cova and Martinez (2025) add that judgments about responsibility under determinism are more complicated than some experimental designs have assumed.

A satisfying theory of free will needs to explain control, not simply point to the absence of determination. If determinism is true, the theory must explain how actions remain attributable to agents. If indeterminism is true, it must explain why choices are not random but still controlled. One promising route treats deliberation, reasons, intentions, and agency as central to human action, while still respecting the constraints the natural world places on us.

It is not possible to settle the free will and determinism debate by simply declaring one side correct. The problem with determinism is real, since it appears to rule out control. But randomness offers no guarantee of freedom either, since indeterminism carries its own serious difficulty. The four sources examined here show why a satisfactory theory has to move past this dichotomy. Van Miltenburg emphasizes intentional and rational agency, Potter and Mitchell question what an absence of determination actually implies for choice, Cova and Martinez show that responsibility judgments persist even under deterministic scenarios, and Tadić shows how far current scientific evidence can and cannot take us. Taken together, these views support a tentative conclusion: human freedom is best understood through meaningful deliberation, reasons, intentions, and control, rather than through the mere absence of causation. The question remains open, but the strongest argument rules out both a fatalistic determinism and an unexamined appeal to randomness as an account of freedom.
