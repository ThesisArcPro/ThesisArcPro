Armed conflict has always tested human rights protections, but modern warfare exposes a troubling reality: international law is strong on paper yet weak in practice. From the bombardment of civilian infrastructure in Ukraine and Gaza to the abuses committed by non-state militias in Sudan and Syria, human rights violations continue largely unchecked despite decades of legal development. This essay argues that the persistence of human rights violations in modern conflict zones stems not from a lack of legal standards, but from a critical enforcement gap, one that spans state actors, non-state armed groups, and the international institutions meant to hold them accountable. Addressing this gap requires more than new treaties; it demands stronger monitoring, broader accountability frameworks, and sustained civil society pressure.

The clearest evidence of this enforcement gap appears in state conduct during active warfare. Rahat and Hasan (2025) examine the conflicts in Ukraine and Gaza through comparative case analysis, identifying repeated violations including assaults on civilian infrastructure, forced deportations, and allegations of collective punishment. These are not isolated incidents but recurring patterns that international human rights law explicitly prohibits under instruments such as the Geneva Conventions and the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights. Yet the mechanisms designed to respond, namely the UN Human Rights Council, the International Criminal Court, and the International Court of Justice, have struggled to translate legal authority into meaningful consequences. Rahat and Hasan found that these bodies are frequently marginalized by geopolitical vetoes within the UN Security Council and by outright non-cooperation from implicated states. A state can be condemned by an international tribunal and still face no binding enforcement, particularly when it holds veto power or enjoys an ally's protection. This dynamic reveals that the problem is structural rather than incidental. International law provides the vocabulary for accountability, but the political architecture surrounding it often prevents that vocabulary from becoming action.

State conduct, however, represents only part of the picture. Many of today's most destructive conflicts are driven by non-state actors, including insurgent groups, national liberation movements, and private military companies, whose legal obligations are less clearly defined than those of sovereign states. Clapham (2006) addresses this gap directly, arguing that despite the traditional focus of human rights law on state duties, non-state actors are increasingly expected to comply with international human rights norms. His analysis of belligerents and armed groups shows that the international community has slowly extended human rights obligations beyond the state, recognizing that insurgencies and militias can commit abuses on a scale comparable to national armies. This matters enormously for modern conflict zones such as Sudan, the Democratic Republic of Congo, and parts of Syria, where non-state militias, rather than formal government forces, are often responsible for mass displacement, extrajudicial killings, and the recruitment of child soldiers. If accountability frameworks remain narrowly focused on states, they will continue to overlook a significant share of the actors actually committing violations. Clapham's argument therefore reinforces the central thesis: the enforcement gap is not simply about weak state compliance, but about an international system that has been slow to adapt its accountability tools to the actual composition of modern warfare.

Beyond individual case studies, broader patterns emerge when examining human rights conditions across multiple conflicts simultaneously. Human Rights Watch's World Report 2016 documents human rights conditions in more than ninety countries and territories, with particular attention to the Syrian civil war and its accompanying humanitarian catastrophe. In his keynote essay, Executive Director Kenneth Roth argues that fear, of terrorism, of refugee movements, and of social media driven unrest, pushed governments in Europe, China, Russia, Ethiopia, and India toward rolling back civil liberties and adopting repressive new laws. This finding is significant because it demonstrates that human rights violations in conflict settings do not occur in isolation. They ripple outward, prompting neighboring and even distant governments to justify their own restrictive measures under the banner of security. The Syrian case in particular illustrates how a single unresolved conflict can generate years of documented abuses, mass displacement, and international paralysis, echoing the same enforcement failures identified in Ukraine and Gaza nearly a decade later. Taken together, these reports show that the enforcement gap is not a temporary or regional anomaly but a persistent feature of the international system across different conflicts, time periods, and geographic regions.

Defenders of restrictive state action often argue that extraordinary security threats justify extraordinary measures, and that human rights protections must sometimes yield to the practical demands of fighting terrorism or insurgency. This argument carries some intuitive appeal, particularly when civilian populations face genuine threats from armed groups. However, this justification does not withstand scrutiny under international law. Clapham's analysis makes clear that human rights obligations apply broadly, extending even to non-state actors engaged in active conflict, which undermines the claim that security concerns can simply override established legal norms. Similarly, Rahat and Hasan demonstrate that international bodies, when properly empowered, are capable of identifying specific violations with precision, including deportations and collective punishment, showing that legal accountability and security operations are not inherently incompatible. The real issue is not that human rights law is too rigid for wartime conditions, but that political will to enforce it is inconsistent. Security concerns may explain why violations occur, but they do not excuse the international community's failure to respond to them.

Addressing this enforcement gap requires action on multiple fronts. Rahat and Hasan (2025) recommend improving monitoring capabilities, ensuring justice and reparations for victims, and strengthening accountability mechanisms within existing international institutions. Ahmadzadeh and Yousefi (2025) extend this argument, contending that reducing human rights violations during armed conflict depends on a combination of legal, political, and humanitarian efforts, rather than legal reform alone. They specifically highlight the importance of enhancing compliance through domestic incorporation of international treaties and promoting robust civil society participation. This combination of top down institutional reform and bottom up civil society pressure offers a more realistic path forward than relying solely on international courts, whose authority remains vulnerable to political interference. Governments that formally incorporate international human rights treaties into domestic law create additional avenues for accountability that do not depend entirely on Security Council cooperation, while active civil society organizations can document abuses and pressure governments even when formal international mechanisms stall.

Human rights violations in modern conflict zones persist not because international law lacks clear standards, but because the mechanisms meant to enforce those standards remain vulnerable to political interference, structurally incomplete in addressing non-state actors, and inconsistently applied across different conflicts and regions. The evidence from Ukraine, Gaza, Syria, and numerous other conflict zones examined across these sources points to a consistent pattern rather than a series of isolated failures. Meaningful progress will require strengthening international accountability mechanisms, extending clear obligations to non-state actors, and empowering domestic legal systems and civil society to fill the gaps left by international institutions. Only through this combined approach can the international community begin closing the enforcement gap that has allowed human rights violations to persist across generations of armed conflict.
