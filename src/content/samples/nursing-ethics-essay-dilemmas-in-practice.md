Nurses make dozens of decisions during every shift, some of which are moral ones that are more than just a checklist or chart. She is not just doing her job when she's asked to sedate an aggressive patient, when someone she's asked to restrain can't do it on their own, or when she has to soften the blow of a terminal diagnosis for a grieving family. She is deciding between two competing goods and, at times, even two competing bad outcomes. This is a moral dilemma at its core: a choice that conflicts with another moral choice. Such problems seldom present themselves overtly or explicitly, and sometimes nurses only become aware in retrospect which principle they emphasized and which they set aside. The tension between patient autonomy, beneficence, justice, and confidentiality and the realities of law, resources, and institutional structure creates ethical challenges in nursing practice. It is vital to grasp these dilemmas and the frameworks nurses employ in their decision-making to preserve patient care and the ethical standards of nursing practice.

Nursing is a unique career in health care. Nurses spend more continuous time at the bedside than any other clinical profession, but often lack the formal authority over treatment decisions as compared to physicians. This proximity and power imbalance enables an ethical landscape to emerge. A nurse can see that a patient may be suffering unnecessarily for their wishes to be realized, or that the wishes are being disregarded before anyone else can. However, she might not have the position she needs to alter the plan. This conflict is expressed directly by Duarte et al. (2023), who stated that Brazilian nursing laws are sometimes not aligned with practice, placing nurses at legal risk if they have to act based on knowledge and skills that are not yet regulated by law. This inequity is not exclusive to Brazil and is found wherever the scope of nursing practice grows more quickly than the enabling legislation or hospital policies for nursing. The four principles of nursing ethics are autonomy (respecting patients' decisions), beneficence (doing what is good for the patient), nonmaleficence (avoiding causing harm to the patient), and justice (caring for patients fairly). All problems that nurses encounter are ultimately a struggle between these four commitments.

The dilemma of patient autonomy versus medical paternalism is probably the most widely known. A nurse can be placed in a difficult situation where she is required to accept a treatment that she feels is lifesaving, while a competent adult has refused it, due to her duty to respect people's decisions and her instinct to promote health. A version of this conflict was recorded by Caro-Alonso et al. (2023) during the COVID-19 pandemic, when nurses in Spain physically restrained patients with intellectual disabilities to prevent them from leaving COVID-19 isolation rooms. Nurses who were interviewed talked about this being the breaking of the autonomy of individuals for the well-being of the community, and they knew it.

The issue of confidentiality is another challenge. Whilst patient confidentiality is always the responsibility of the nurse, there are situations where disclosure to a family member, a partner, or a public health authority may be required in order to prevent harm. Whether the duty to warn outweighs the duty to protect privacy is a judgment that is not fully prepared for by any policy manual.

One type of care that presents some of the most emotionally challenging situations that nurses may face is end-of-life care. When it comes to do-not-resuscitate orders, withdrawal of life support, and differing opinions among family members about a patient's wishes, nurses become central decision makers in choices they didn't choose to make. The study by Caro-Alonso et al. (2023) revealed that nurses sometimes reduced the information they shared when contacted by phone to inform relatives of the death of a patient with COVID-19. This situation did not leave them feeling comfortable with the lack of honesty.

Dilemmas of resource allocation and short-staffing also arise. In situations where beds, ventilators, or staff time are limited, nurses have to make decisions, either through their actions or words, about who gets care first. The same study found that age was sometimes used as a triage method adopted early in the pandemic, reserving care for younger patients. Nurses commented that they saw the principle of justice being violated because of events beyond their sphere of influence.

Issues of informed consent are also complicated, especially where language barriers, cognitive impairment, or reduced capacity due to age or illness exist. An additional layer to this is that newly licensed nurses and nursing students might be hesitant to challenge more experienced nurses when they feel that something is not right, according to Hwu and Pai (2025). This matters because it influences what nurses tell patients, as well as what they believe they can speak out for on the patient's behalf. This hierarchy adds to the complexity of the consent and disclosure process.

Nurses do not often have time for lengthy deliberations, and they rely on frameworks to make swift, consistent, and reasoned decisions about dilemmas. The approach centered on autonomy, beneficence, nonmaleficence, and justice is the most commonly taught and used, and provides a common language for considering conflicting arguments. These principles are translated into specific professional responsibilities in national codes of ethics from nursing associations. Hospital ethics committees offer another layer of support, available to give advice where a case is too complex or too controversial to be dealt with at a unit or nurse level alone.

Khosravani et al. (2024) examined this at the management level, gathering data on the ethical issues embedded in the management of daily activities by conducting interviews with twenty nurse managers in Iran. These results demonstrate that framework knowledge is not limited to care at the bedside. Clear knowledge of ethical principles also boosted the confidence of managers in handling conflicts between staffing, disciplinary measures, and patient complaints while these were considered in the context of institutional pressures. Use of these frameworks remains reliant on organizational culture and leadership support. This implies that an ethical approach works best when ethics principles are shared at all levels of a nursing organization and not limited to nurses working on the front line.

If an ethical situation isn't resolved, or if it's not resolved properly, the cost can mount rapidly. Caro-Alonso et al. (2023) directly associated unresolved ethical conflict with moral distress, as nurses reported guilt, helplessness, and frustration years after a challenging shift. Left untreated, this stress can lead to burnout, and over time, experienced nurses can become frustrated and leave the clinical environment they once enjoyed. Legal threats also exist. When laws are ambiguous, nurses can find themselves liable even if they acted in good faith, cautions Duarte et al. (2023). Beyond the individual nurse, the consequences of poor ethical navigation include the loss of patient trust in the healthcare system and the erosion of the therapeutic relationship that nursing care is built upon. These downstream harms must be guarded against through institutional support: ethics consultation, structured debriefing, and training.

Ethical issues don't just happen out of the blue in nursing; they are an integral part of the field. With each shift, some form of the struggle between autonomy and safety, honesty and compassion, individual care and collective justice arises. Even when using frameworks such as principlism, nurses have a starting point but not a cushion that absorbs the emotional burden of these decisions. The kind of preparation that must take place should include ethics, communication skills, and institutional support, from the new student to the chief management team, as Hwu and Pai (2025) propose. Pairing new nurses with experienced nurses can help close this gap early. Ultimately, nursing ethics is not a subject in which one is supposed to eliminate the problems. It's about developing the discernment and the strength of the structures that support nurses, so they can deal with these dilemmas effectively.
