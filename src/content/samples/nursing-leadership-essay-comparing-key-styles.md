The actions of nursing leaders directly impact patient safety, staff morale and the quality of patient care provided in health care delivery systems. Nurse leaders are being asked to navigate a range of complex conditions, including a lack of staff, changing clinical needs, and high pressure, in their work with varied teams. There is no one-size-fits-all leadership style that is applicable to all situations nurse leaders face. Rather, there are multiple styles, such as transformational, transactional, autocratic and servant leadership, which provide nurse leaders with a variety of tools to address a specific clinical context. This essay will discuss the four leadership styles and their main attributes, advantages and disadvantages in the context of nursing. It suggests that successful nurse leaders are not always a specific type of leader, but are flexible with their style based on the clinical situation they face.

<!--split-->

Transformational leadership is about inspiring and motivating employees to achieve a shared vision of the future by providing mentoring and caring for employees as a means of supporting individual growth. The nurse leader embraces innovation, sets a good example for ethical behavior, and invests in the professional growth of staff. In their systematic review, Niinihuhta and Häggman-Laitila (2022) found that transformational leadership showed the highest positive correlations with measurable aspects of the wellbeing of nurses compared to all other styles they examined, including authentic, resonant and servant. Similarly, Alluhaybi et al. (2023) identified transformational behaviours as being closely associated with increased staff work engagement, such as increased dedication and vigor, amongst their sample of nurses from a variety of healthcare environments.

The benefits of transformational leadership are widely known. It helps to reduce burnout, increases trust, and creates an atmosphere of appreciation instead of management. But this style also has its drawbacks. Transformational leadership requires building relationships and trust, and this takes time, leading to less immediate applicability in emergencies or acute crises. It also demands strong interpersonal and communication skills, a trait some nurse leaders do not have. A nurse manager leading a unit through a major change is likely to need to exercise transformational leadership to gain staff buy-in. At the same time, a sudden surge of patients would require another level of leadership.

<!--split-->

Transactional leadership is based on a set of clearly defined expectations, a clear system of supervision, and a system of rewards or corrective action based on performance. This style focuses on efficiency, consistency and accountability, not on emotional attachment and long-term vision. According to Moreira et al. (2026), the second most frequent leadership style among Portuguese nurses was the transactional style, and the two styles were positively related, implying that transactional behaviors are not necessarily in conflict with transformational leadership, but are in fact often used in conjunction with it. Their study revealed that the most powerful aspect of transactional leadership among the nurses studied was contingent reward, which involves acknowledging and rewarding good performance.

The positive aspect of transactional leadership is its application in achieving regulatory requirements and standardization of clinical protocols. This structured process is useful for tasks like medication administration routines, shift assignments and compliance audits. But there are real drawbacks to transactional leadership. If used too strictly, it can stifle creativity and lower nurses' sense of autonomy, and it doesn't do much to foster long-term commitment the way transformational leadership does. Most effective nurse leaders combine transactional and more relational approaches to leadership rather than relying on the former for everyday management of activities.

Autocratic leadership involves decision-making at the top, with the leader assuming most or all authority and little opportunity for input from employees. This style is not collaborative, but emphasizes quickness and decisiveness. Al Thawabiya et al. (2023) conducted a study on nurse leaders in Qatar that revealed that directors of nursing generally displayed transformational leadership qualities. Still, the head nurses who were closer to the bedside showed more autocratic qualities, suggesting that the nearer a nurse leader was to fast-paced clinical demands, the more autocratic they tended to be.

<!--split-->

The main advantage of an autocratic style of leadership is its effectiveness during emergencies. In a code blue, rapid response event, or mass casualty situation, there is no time for group discussion, and one strong voice can save lives. In non-crisis situations, this same quality becomes a hindrance. Reduced trust in management, demotivation of staff members, and feelings of being undervalued or demoralized by exclusion from decisions that affect their work are all qualities associated with sustained autocratic leadership. This can lead to increased staff turnover and reduced morale over time. So autocratic leadership is best applied as a situational management style rather than the norm, used when the need for fast decisions and clarity takes precedence over the value of input and collaboration.

The concept of servant leadership is the opposite of traditional leadership, which centers on the leader's authority and recognition; servant leadership instead looks first at the needs, growth and wellbeing of team members. Nurse leaders who practice this style focus more on listening, empathy and support than on command. A study by Alluhaybi et al. (2023) revealed that servant leadership and the other relational models, transformational, authentic, resonant and ethical, were associated with greater positive nurse engagement practices than distant or directive leadership practices.

Because it builds strong trust and provides support to promote ethical and patient-centered cultures, servant leadership is particularly valuable when mentoring new graduate nurses and combating burnout amongst experienced nurses. It also helps enhance long-term staff retention, as nurses feel genuinely supported. One constraint on servant leadership is speed. Because attending to individual needs and building consensus takes time, this style will not always be feasible; in situations of acute time pressure or chronic understaffing, top-down direction may be needed to help maintain safe and on-time patient care.

<!--split-->

These four styles can be compared to show a consistent tension between speed and collaboration, short-term compliance and long-term engagement. Transformational and servant leadership are gradual processes that take time to establish trust and wellbeing, but they slow down in situations that demand urgency. Transactional and autocratic leadership are quick and structured, but they can lead to disconnection with employees if not balanced with other approaches. However, these styles are not mutually exclusive; based on the positive correlation Moreira et al. (2026) found between transformational and transactional leadership styles, nurse leaders tend to use a combination of approaches as required at any given time. This reflects a situational perspective of leadership that holds there is no single superior method of leadership for everyone. A good nurse leader combines leadership styles, operating in an autocratic mode during crises, using a transactional style for routine compliance, and drawing on a transformational or servant style for morale and retention.

Leadership style in nursing is not a fixed identity but a set of tools that should be matched to the demands of the situation at hand. Transformational leadership fosters trust and wellbeing over time; transactional leadership establishes structure and accountability; autocratic leadership provides direction when time is limited; and servant leadership sustains morale and retention. The sources presented here illustrate that good nurse leaders incorporate a mix of these strategies instead of relying on one approach alone. Education and leadership development programs for nurses should therefore focus on adaptability, helping nursing leaders become familiar with the skills of each approach as needed for different situations. This flexibility has a direct impact on improved patient care, stronger teams, and a healthier nursing workforce.
