Healthcare has been slowly moving from a disease-centric approach to a person-centered one, where the person is the focus of all clinical decisions. This change is because of a growing awareness that a diagnosis is not a treatment for a person. The framework that has emerged to guide this shift is patient-centered care (PCC), which centers on the importance of respect, communication, and shared decision-making with the patient and provider. Nurses, who interact with patients more than any other member of the health care team, are in a unique position to help bring this philosophy into practice. This essay examines essential tenets of patient-centered care, the specialized contribution nurses can make to the delivery of patient-centred care, the evidence to support the connection between patient-centred care and enhanced patient outcomes, and the current challenges impeding the widespread implementation of patient-centred care in healthcare settings. This analysis supports the claim made by recent nursing and public health research that patient-centered care is not just about the bedside manner, but an approach with measurable impacts on the patient and the health system as a whole.

<!--split-->

Patient-centered care is more broadly defined as not just treating the medical condition, but the whole person, including physical, psychological, and social needs. Wasim, Sajan, and Majid (2023) state that researchers and healthcare professionals typically define PCC as both addressing the patient as a whole person and, as a key aspect of PCC, engaging in shared decision making. With the aim of identifying the current status of government-led PCC initiatives in nine developed countries, they conducted an environmental scan of these programs that revealed that, though the concept has been widely adopted, there is no single definition, and its linked relationship with other concepts like patient and family engagement has not been clearly understood. There are significant gaps in the understanding of how to implement PCC programs. This is not a lack of value; it is simply a result of the ambiguity. Instead, it refers to a model where there are consistent values such as respect for patient preferences, coordinating care across the care team, emotional support, clear and honest communication, and continuity of care as the person moves through care settings. These principles are important to have as a foundation for analyzing nursing practice.

Nurses are constantly, directly involved with the patient during an episode of illness and thus are on the frontline of the delivery of patient-centered care. Narayan (2022) carried out in-depth interviews with twenty home health nurses, who expressed that patient-centered care was closely related to being "genuinely invested" in the individual patient, rather than in a case to be managed. Remarkably, the study also found that most of these nurses learned to do things for their patients through trial and error instead of learning it in nursing school or induction programs. This finding is important because it underscores the fact that all the attributes of patient-centered nursing, such as empathy, careful listening, and individualized planning, are learned skills that are not systematically developed. These findings are nuanced by Ferla et al. (2023), who conducted a cross-sectional study of 411 Brazilian health professionals using the Patient-Practitioner Orientation Scale (PPOS). They found that nurses performed moderately well on the caring element of the instrument, but quite poorly on the sharing element, which reflects how much nurses involve patients in decision-making and how much information they share. This gap indicates that nurse-patient relationships are likely strong on the emotional dimension, but that collaborative decision-making may not yet have replaced a more traditional and directive approach, perhaps because of the continuing influence of the biomedical model in nursing education and practice.

<!--split-->

The relationship between patient-centered care and measurable health outcomes has gained increasing attention in nursing and public health studies. Yu et al. (2023) conducted a cross-sectional study of 5,222 inpatients in China to investigate the association between patient-centered care and patients' self-reported physical and mental health status, perceived need for hospitalization, and overutilization of unnecessary services by physicians. They found that the use of more patient-centered practices is linked to improvements in self-reported health and less self-reported unnecessary care. This finding is important because it indicates that the improvement in subjective satisfaction is not a trivial effect of PCC. It appears to affect both the appropriateness of healthcare resource use and patients' own understanding of their illness. The authors also situate their results within a growing body of research suggesting that PCC can help lower information barriers between patients and providers, enabling patients to make more active decisions about their healthcare. But the relationship is not positive in every context. Other studies within the same research literature suggest that PCC effects may not be uniform across all patient populations, and that a single PCC approach might not produce the same result in every clinical setting. This is a key consideration for nurses and healthcare administrators, who must adapt patient-focused practices to account for differences among populations instead of applying a one-size-fits-all model. It also points to the need for more longitudinal studies, since a single cross-sectional survey, no matter how large, cannot fully capture the impact of patient-centered practices on outcomes over time or across repeated hospital visits during a chronic illness.

Although it has many positive aspects, patient-centered care faces several ongoing challenges in nursing practice. One major constraint is time. In the face of heavy workloads, nurses may find it challenging to engage in the kind of conversation that patient-centered care requires, which is deeply personalized and time-consuming. Academic level was also found to be a significant predictor of greater patient-centered attitudes, indicating that further nursing education and specialized training could increase the use of patient-centeredness, but this requires investment that may not always be available to a nursing unit. This concern is supported by the research of Narayan (2022): patient-centered skills are often acquired informally rather than taught formally, resulting in inconsistent practice across the profession. Wasim et al. (2023) also note that cultural sensitivity training is an important but underutilized aspect of patient-centered models, since nurses caring for a multicultural clientele need to be aware of varying cultural expectations regarding communication, autonomy, and family involvement in decision-making. Institutional and systemic resistance also play a role. Time-pressured, efficiency-driven health systems may inadvertently discourage the relationship building that is key to patient-centered care. A challenge that will continue is balancing patient autonomy with clinical guidelines, especially when a patient's preference conflicts with recommended treatment. In these cases, nurses must play a mediating role, conveying clinical reasoning to patients while still respecting their right to make choices about their own treatment.
<!--split-->
Patient-centered care is more than a concept. It is a practical, evidence-based strategy that reshapes how nurses build relationships and share information, and it helps improve patient outcomes. The findings across these four studies are consistent in several ways: nurses play a major role in delivering patient-centered care, that care is evident in some degree of caring behavior, and its presence is measurably linked to both health outcomes and healthcare resource utilization. At the same time, a lack of formal training, inconsistent definitions, and systemic pressures continue to limit how fully this model is implemented in daily practice. Going forward, healthcare systems need to invest more deliberately in teaching patient-centered skills, rather than leaving nurses to learn them through individual experience alone. Doing so would help patient-centered care become a routine practice rather than something that depends on individual effort. The health, safety, and responsiveness of any healthcare system depend on how it treats and cares for the people within it.
