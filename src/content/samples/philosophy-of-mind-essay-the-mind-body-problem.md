The mind-body problem asks how mental life relates to physical reality. Thoughts, feelings, intentions, perceptions, and conscious experiences appear distinct from measurable brain processes, yet changes to the brain can transform memory, personality, perception, and awareness. The central question is whether subjective consciousness can be fully explained in physical terms alone. Mørch (2023) shows how consciousness poses a genuine challenge to physicalism while surveying the range of non-physicalist alternatives available. This essay argues that dualism captures the distinctive quality of conscious experience, but that its problems with interaction and systematicity weaken its explanatory power. Physicalism and functionalism offer more coherent accounts of how mind depends on and is organized by the body, yet neither adequately explains how physical or computational processes give rise to conscious experience itself.

The gap between mind and body appears in two areas. Physical states are spatial, measurable, and causal; mental states are subjective and carry intentional content. Neural activity can be correlated with pain, for instance, but the felt quality of pain is more than a description of firing neurons. Mørch (2023) identifies this explanatory gap as a central obstacle for physicalism. The difficulty deepens given the apparent causal significance of mental events: decisions give rise to bodily actions, and bodily injuries give rise to conscious pain. Any adequate theory must explain both dependence and difference without resorting to an implausible account of causation. This tension makes consciousness a uniquely demanding test case for any theory that claims to explain the mind purely in terms of physical mechanisms and objective description.

<!--split-->

Substance dualism offers one answer by treating mind and body as two distinct kinds of substance. In the Cartesian tradition, the mind is a thinking, non-extended substance, while the body is physical and extended. Its appeal lies in preserving the distinctiveness of conscious experience rather than reducing thoughts and feelings to purely physical description. As Mørch (2023) explains, the difficulty of accounting for phenomenal consciousness in strictly physical terms continues to motivate theories that are not exclusively physicalist. Dualism therefore takes seriously the intuition that a complete account of physical processes may still fail to capture what an experience feels like from the inside.

Substance dualism, however, faces a well-known problem of interaction. If the mind is non-physical and the body is physical, it is difficult to explain how one could causally affect the other. The close correlation between brain states and mental changes further challenges a theory that separates the two so sharply. Saad (2024) shows that contemporary dualism can be more nuanced than the traditional Cartesian picture; his discussion of "tracking" dualism considers how experiences might relate to physical conditions without assuming an unexplained causal link between two distinct substances. Even so, this refinement does not resolve every difficulty. Saad points to a broader systematicity problem: the question of how psychophysical relations should be structured in a principled way. Dualism offers valuable insight into consciousness, but it commits its defenders to an ambitious theory of the psychophysical connection.

<!--split-->

Physicalism starts from the opposite assumption: that physical reality is all there is, and that mental phenomena must ultimately be explained in physical terms. This view fits naturally with a background in neuroscience, since brain injury, medication, stimulation, and neurological disease can all transform conscious life, suggesting a close relationship between consciousness and physical organization. Mørch (2023) explains that physicalism draws support from this dependence and from causal closure arguments: if physical events already have sufficient physical causes, then adding distinct mental causes seems redundant or creates unwanted competition between causes.

Identity theory offers a stronger version of this idea by equating mental states directly with particular brain states, providing a straightforward account of mental causation. Even so, the problem of phenomenal consciousness remains difficult for physicalists. Explaining neural activity does not, by itself, explain why that processing is accompanied by a subjective point of view. Mørch (2023) raises explanatory, conceivability, and knowledge-based challenges that question whether complete physical knowledge would automatically yield complete knowledge of experience.

Baysan and Wildman (2024) complicate the debate further. Rather than treating physicalism and anti-physicalism as a strict binary, they defend a disjunctive position on which consciousness may be grounded in either physical or non-physical properties depending on the case. Their argument suggests that contemporary philosophy need not force a stark choice between two rigid metaphysical camps. Despite the explanatory strength of physicalism, the metaphysical status of consciousness remains contested because physicalism's explanatory reach still has limits.

<!--split-->

Functionalism offers a third path between substance dualism and strict identity theory. It identifies mental states with their causal and computational roles rather than with any particular material composition, defining a mental state by its inputs, internal interactions, and outputs. On this view, the same type of mental function could, in principle, be realized by different physical systems.

Wiese (2024) examines this question through artificial consciousness and the free energy principle, distinguishing genuine consciousness from processes that merely simulate it. His analysis suggests that reproducing a system's causal organization does not guarantee that the relevant functions, and the experience they might support, are reproduced as well. Wiese's proposed criteria for consciousness emphasize implementation, energy dynamics, causal flow, and existential conditions, with particular weight given to causal organization. This poses a challenge for a purely functionalist approach: matching functional roles may not be sufficient to duplicate consciousness itself. Functionalism improves on strict identity theory by allowing multiple physical realizations of the same mental state, but it still struggles to fully account for subjective experience.

All three approaches illuminate different aspects of the problem. Dualism is strongest in insisting on the apparent irreducibility of subjective experience, but weakest in explaining psychophysical interaction and systematic structure. Physicalism offers the most direct connection to neuroscience by dispensing with any distinct mental substance, yet it faces the explanatory problem of how physical processes could feel like anything from the inside. Functionalism is more flexible than strict identity theory, framing mental states in terms of organization and causal role, but as Wiese (2024) shows, functional or computational similarity alone can still fail to capture genuine consciousness.

It would therefore be a mistake to reduce this debate to a simple choice of winner. As Baysan and Wildman's (2024) disjunctive argument illustrates, the opposition between physicalism and anti-physicalism may be more complicated than it is often presented. Similarly, Mørch (2023) shows that several non-physicalist theories remain viable alternatives to purely physical explanation. Saad's (2024) discussion of dualism further suggests that some dualist positions survive even after the traditional Cartesian account of interaction is rejected. The conclusion is comparative rather than decisive: physicalist and functionalist accounts of dependence and organization are more coherent overall, but neither fully explains subjective consciousness.

The mind-body problem persists because information about brain activity does not amount to information about conscious experience. Substance dualism highlights the distinctive character of mental life, but its problems with interaction and systematicity limit its explanatory power. Physicalism accounts for the mind's dependence on the brain, yet it struggles to explain phenomenal consciousness in purely material terms. Functionalism adds an emphasis on causal organization, though Wiese's analysis shows that computational simulation is not necessarily equivalent to lived experience. Taken together, these four sources suggest that none of these theories fully resolves the problem on its own. The physicalist and functionalist frameworks remain the most coherent available, yet consciousness still points to something that current explanations of brain and behavior have not fully captured.
