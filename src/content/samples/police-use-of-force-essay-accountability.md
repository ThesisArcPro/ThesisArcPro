The use of force by police officers is a topic of significant debate within criminology and law enforcement, impacting public safety, civil liberties, and trust in the police. There are legal reasons for using force when necessary, but there are also many implications to its use that are ethical and legal. In incidents that touch on the question of reasonableness or excessiveness of force, public concern is likely to increase. Policing, on the other hand, is a set of unpredictable encounters that often necessitate officers' quick decision-making in stressful situations. These conflicting realities show that neither totalitarian police nor excessive restrictions has proved itself to be a good solution for society. Rather, effective policing must strike a balance that empowers police officers to intervene in extreme circumstances whilst also ensuring they are held to account via clear oversight, evidence-informed policies, and ongoing professional development.

Police officers are regularly confronted with violent offenders, armed suspects, and individuals with mental health crises. During these interactions, hesitation or poor performance could result in endangering officers and innocent civilians. That's why criminal justice systems acknowledge that officers are entitled to respond with force if and when that is necessary and proportionate to the threat. The use of force continuum is a reflection of this principle, and they should start with verbal and non-violent methods of control, move to less-lethal, and then deadly force only if justified.

The evaluation of police decisions can sometimes be difficult, as officers have limited time to evaluate rapidly changing threats. According to Di Nota et al. (2021), the force evaluation shall take into account objective evidence, as well as subjective human factors such as stress, perception, and cognitive workload. These factors affect officers' perception of danger and reaction to stress. Likewise, Nix (2020) maintains that the context of each use of force incident is unique, as circumstances vary greatly between encounters. Police need to have the necessary powers to safeguard the public. Still, those powers need to be subject to proper and accessible legal frameworks and effective control so that there is no disproportionate or unnecessary use of force.

Police powers are vital, but so too is accountability when they are not exercised or when they are misused or lack proper review. Low public trust when investigations are inconsistent, disciplinary processes are not transparent, and agencies do not have reliable information on use-of-force incidents. Nix (2020) notes that researchers continue to encounter great challenges given the lack of and inconsistency with national data on police deadly force. If there isn't a standardized reporting system, it is hard for policymakers to identify any trends of misconduct or whether reforms are making a difference.

Lack of accountability systems also makes the organization challenging. DeWitt et al. (2023) list the challenges as a lack of policy consistency, supervision, organizational culture, and transparency. Such weaknesses lead to low levels of public confidence and hinder agencies in their learning from past incidents. In addition, communities may have doubts about the fairness and objectivity of investigations due to inconsistent body camera policies and limited independent oversight. Accountability not only should be a way to tackle misconduct, but also a platform to enhance the legitimacy of law enforcement institutions. Consistency in the behavioral standards of officers helps improve cooperation with police and trust in the justice system.

Practical reforms that will enhance police performance and public trust in the police are essential for effective accountability. There is one key measure that has been promoted as an effective way to increase independent oversight: civilian review boards or external investigative systems. Independent review serves as a way to support the fairness of complaints and minimize perceptions of bias in the investigation.

There is also an important role for technology. Body-worn cameras and clear reporting practices offer important evidence to help with objective analysis of police encounters. Di Nota et al. (2021) suggest that objective measures, like video footage, need to be taken into account with knowledge of officer decision-making to create balanced assessments. These tools help to ensure the safety of officers and the public, and help to document an incident.

Training is yet another critical reform. DeWitt et al. (2023) suggest placing a stronger focus on de-escalation, communication, leadership, and officer wellness to minimize the need for unnecessary force and ensure public safety. Scenario exercises can enhance the ability of officers to make decisions effectively when facing stressful situations and help them defuse violence where possible. Early intervention systems also increase accountability by tracking and flagging problematic behaviors as they occur and before they become a serious disciplinary problem.

Bennell et al. (2021) suggest that police departments need to adopt evidence-based practices that have been substantiated by interdisciplinary research. More interaction between researchers, policies, psychology, and law enforcement professions can lead to better force training, better policies, and more consistent methods of assessing force. These reforms are based on the principle that accountability should be for continuous improvement, not punishment.

Some critics have suggested that more oversight may lead officers to hesitate in making the appropriate decisions in high-risk situations. They argue that their hesitation might result from fear of investigation or, in the event of public criticism, be a risk to officers and civilians. Such concerns should be seriously considered because making decisions under highly stressful situations is often the role performed by police officers.

Effective accountability does not, however, rule out the use of lawful and necessary force by officers. Rather, it sets apart the ethical behavior from the wrong behavior, and it upholds professional ethics. In their article, Di Nota et al. (2021) illustrate how the psychological needs of policing can be used to evaluate the job accurately, instead of criticizing it. In the same way, DeWitt et al. (2023) point out that effective accountability systems promote organizational learning, increase supervision, and boost community trust. Instead of reducing policing, accountability promotes evidence-driven, consistent decision-making that is good for cops and citizens.

The use of force by police will be a key yet highly monitored component of contemporary policing. Police should have enough powers to ensure the safety of communities and deal with unsafe situations, but with clear accountability systems to ensure fairness and trust in the community. Collecting data, providing independent oversight, wearing body cameras, training police, and reforming their practices contribute to better policing. As Nix (2020), Di Nota et al. (2021), DeWitt et al. (2023), and Bennell et al. (2021) all show, authority and accountability are not mutually exclusive aims. They form a criminal justice system that is more effective at ensuring public safety, protecting individual rights, and building trust between police and communities.
